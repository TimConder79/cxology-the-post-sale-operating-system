Chapter 20

Health Scoring That Actually Works

If your health score only signals a problem right before renewal, you don't have a health score — you have an autopsy report.

In the previous chapter, we established that a Single Source of Truth and clean data are the prerequisites for scale. Now, it is time to put that data to work.

Health scoring is the ultimate operational lever for prioritizing your team's resources and predicting revenue. However, in many organizations, health scores are a source of immense frustration. They are often bloated, overly complex, and filled with lagging indicators that only signal a customer is at risk when it is far too late to save the account. If your health score relies entirely on a massive drop in software usage right before a renewal, you don't have a predictive health score—you have an autopsy report.

To build a health score that actually predicts reality and drives proactive interventions, you must adopt the Strategic Customer Success approach to measurement.

The Foundation: Avoidable vs. Unavoidable Churn

The single biggest mistake companies make when building a health score is trying to encapsulate every single variable that has ever caused a customer to leave. This makes the score mathematically bloated and operationally useless.

To fix this, you must fiercely separate churn into two distinct categories and entirely ignore one of them:

  • Unavoidable Churn: This is churn over which your company has absolutely no control. Examples include a customer going out of business, being acquired, or "graduating" from your solution because they grew into an enterprise corporation. You cannot build a predictive health score for a company being acquired..
  • Avoidable Churn: This is churn your company directly contributed to. It is the result of slow or painful onboarding, poor communication, technical glitches, or a fundamental misalignment between what sales promised and what your product actually delivers.

The Rule: Your health score must focus exclusively on avoidable churn. By eliminating the noise of unavoidable churn, your score instantly becomes a more accurate reflection of the relationship dynamics you actually control.

The Inputs: Traditional vs. Growth-Oriented Criteria

A health score that actually works requires a balanced mix of two different types of criteria. Relying solely on one will fail to tell the whole story of the customer.

Traditional Criteria (The Baseline) These are the standard metrics most companies use: software usage, Net Promoter Score (NPS), the quantity of support cases, and the upcoming renewal date. While necessary, these are often lagging indicators. A drop in usage alone doesn't tell you much until it's paired with deeper context. To make traditional criteria effective, apply specificity—such as measuring product usage in key features rather than overall logins.

Growth-Oriented Criteria (The Predictors) Growth-oriented criteria measure activity that often happens outside of the product itself, providing leading indicators of future success. These include:

  • Relationship Reach: Are you getting more executives and teams interested in your product, or are you dangerously reliant on a single internal champion?
  • Lifecycle Progression: Are they progressing through your plays as expected? Did they achieve First Value on time, and have they successfully completed their recent Goal Blocks?
  • Customer Engagement: Are they participating in your marketing efforts, acting as a reference, or evangelizing their accomplishments within their own company?
  • Sophistication of Use: Are they transitioning from an apprentice to a master by adopting your more advanced features? Tracking "Sophistication of Use" is how a CS team mathematically proves the iterative approach is working. It measures the customer's journey of continuously coming back to solve harder problems, overcome challenges, and build new capabilities, which is the ultimate leading indicator of retention and product stickiness.

Modeling Success

When companies finally sit down to assign values to their health score, they usually start by analyzing why their past customers left. This is the wrong approach.

The best health scores are not built by reverse-engineering failure; they are built by reverse-engineering your healthiest customers. You want to identify what works and replicate that across your entire customer base. To build your scoring model, select a sample of your absolute best customers and ask your team: What does a healthy customer actually look like?

  • Alignment & Champions: Do we have alignment across their team with a strong internal champion? Are they able to clearly communicate their business objectives?
  • Skill Sets & Habits: Do they have the right tactical skills to execute efficiently, and do they demonstrate good habits without over-configuring the technology?
  • Engagement: Are they present and engaged for our meetings, or do they consistently reschedule? Do they follow through on their commitments and utilize self-service materials?
  • Advocacy: Are they a source of referrals, or did they achieve an outcome so impressive it became a case study?

Evaluating, Defining, and Weighting Criteria

Once you identify the core behaviors of your healthiest customers, you must narrow them down to a focused list of about six criteria.

Evaluate the Criteria: Ask yourself three critical questions to ensure these metrics are actually viable:

  • What do we expect to learn by measuring this metric?
  • What can we do with that knowledge?
  • When will we measure it, and how will we collect the data to assign a score?

Define the Score: For your team to gather data effectively, you must write a clear description defining the intent of the criteria and exactly what constitutes a "high score" versus a "low score".

Weight the Score: Not all criteria have the same predictive power. You must weight your metrics so they add up to 100% based on their actual correlation to customer health. For example, if you notice that 85% of your churned customers had poor product usage, but only 40% had a low CSM pulse score, product usage is a significantly stronger predictor and must be given a higher weight in your model.

The Roll-Out Process

Many health scores fail because teams overcomplicate them and hesitate to launch. To successfully roll out your new health score, treat it like a strategic play and follow these five steps:

  • Design & Prototype: Take a small sampling of customers and try your new scoring model out to see how it feels.
  • The Initial Pass: Once confident in the prototype, execute an initial pass across your entire customer base, involving your team early so they understand the criteria.
  • Review for Insights: Take time to review the initial pass and see what broad insights you can glean about your portfolio's overall health.
  • Validate the Extremes: Look at your highest "green" accounts and your lowest "red" accounts. Discuss with your team: Is this an accurate representation of reality? Use this discussion to refine and improve the score.
  • Share the Value: Once you are confident the score accurately reflects reality, tell others. Share the insights with Sales, Product, and the C-suite so the entire company can consume and act on the data.

Do not let your health score go stale, and above all, do not hesitate. A simple, live health score that inspires action is always better than a perfect score that never launches.

Chapter 21: Cross-Team Collaboration KPIs

With a Single Source of Truth established and a predictive health score actively running, you now possess something incredibly powerful: hard, reliable data. However, if that data stays trapped within the Customer Success department, you will never realize its full potential.

By design, many companies suffer from a natural tension between departments. Sales focuses on acquiring new logos, Product focuses on shipping features, Marketing focuses on generating leads, and Customer Success is often left holding the bag for non-customer-centric decisions. When this friction becomes toxic, silos form, trust erodes, and the customer ultimately pays the price.

To break down these silos and achieve true, scalable growth, you must transition from departmental goals to shared, cross-team Key Performance Indicators (KPIs). In 2026, aligning the C-suite, Sales, Product, and CS through shared metrics is a foundational requirement for operational maturity.

Moving Beyond Basic NPS

For years, companies relied heavily on Net Promoter Score (NPS) or Customer Satisfaction (CSAT) as their primary cross-company indicators of customer happiness. While these metrics have their place, they are lagging indicators that fail to align the business monetarily. You cannot pay your employees with NPS points.

To bridge the gap between the C-suite and the CS team, you must speak the language of the business: finance and operational efficiency. You must clear the fog with data and become financial experts in the value of retention.

Modern cross-team collaboration relies on three critical shared KPIs:

Shared Metric 1: Net Revenue Retention (NRR) NRR is the percentage of recurring revenue retained from existing customers over a defined period, including upsells, downgrades, and churn. It is the ultimate shared KPI because it is the most significant driver of high company valuations. NRR cannot be achieved by Customer Success alone; it requires Marketing to attract the right leads, Sales to set the right expectations, Product to deliver functioning features, and CS to drive value realization. When the entire C-suite shares the NRR goal, retention transforms from a "CS problem" into a whole-company objective.

Shared Metric 2: Forecast Accuracy As Customer Success evolves into a revenue engine, CS teams must own expansion targets and forecasting with the same rigor that Sales applies to net-new business. Tracking the accuracy of your post-sale revenue forecasts (predicting renewals and expansions) aligns CS directly with Sales Operations and the CFO. When CS can accurately forecast revenue based on health scores and completed Value Blocks, they earn their seat at the strategic table.

Shared Metric 3: Cycle Time Reduction Cycle time reduction measures the speed and efficiency of moving a customer from one phase to the next. For cross-team collaboration, the most important cycle time is the transition from "Closed-Won" to First Value. This shared metric forces Sales and CS to collaborate on a seamless Internal Knowledge Transfer (IKT), ensuring the customer does not lose momentum or have to repeat themselves after the deal is signed.

Operationalizing Alignment: Joint Reviews

Establishing shared KPIs is only effective if you actively review them together. You must institute recurring joint reviews to ensure your teams remain in lockstep.

CS and Sales Alignment Sales and CS should not operate as separate entities; they are two halves of the same revenue engine. In joint reviews, these teams must evaluate the quality of the IKT, discuss upcoming renewal risks, and strategize on expansion timing. When CS identifies a highly successful customer through their health score, they must feed that story back to Sales to help them close future deals faster by selling "recreatable successes".

CS and Product Alignment Product leaders make decisions that impact all customers, and they can sometimes be skeptical if CS brings them anecdotal complaints. Joint reviews with Product must be driven by data. CS should share feature utilization trends, report on product-driven churn risks, and validate the product roadmap using real customer stories and health score trends.

CS and Marketing Alignment Marketing teams are constantly searching for compelling stories. When CS effectively executes the "Sharing Insights" and "Alignment Meeting" plays, they generate a goldmine of customer wins. In joint reviews, CS should provide Marketing with successful "Win Reports" to fuel powerful case studies, referenceable customers, and targeted campaigns.

3. The C-Suite Connection

Finally, you must use these shared KPIs to continuously educate your C-suite and board of directors. Many executives suffer from an "optimistic leadership" bias, prioritizing short-term customer acquisition over the long-term realities of customer retention.

By consistently presenting NRR, forecast accuracy, and the actual cost of avoidable churn to your leadership team, you frame the complexities of Customer Success in terms everyone values. This data-driven approach removes emotion from the equation, proving that strategic retention is the cheapest, most efficient way to grow the business.
