Chapter 12

Play 4 — The First Value Play

First Value is not Go Live. Go Live is your company's win. First Value is the customer's win.

Following the Six Motions of Onboarding, the customer takes the wheel and drives toward their first critical milestone. For years, the software industry has focused heavily on the concept of "Go Live"—the moment the product is fully implemented. However, the modern Customer Success engine focuses on an entirely different outcome: First Value.

1. The Why: The Heart, The Smart, and The Minimum Viable Win

A primary reason onboarding fails is that companies confuse "Go Live" with First Value. "Go Live" is your company's win; it means the software is turned on. First Value, on the other hand, is the moment the customer actually feels they have achieved something meaningful from their purchase.

To understand the difference, look at how Disney manages its theme park lines. The customer's decision to join the queue and wait for the ride is "Go Live", but being entertained by characters or interactive games while standing in that line is First Value. It demonstrates progress and value long before the ultimate outcome is reached.

In SaaS, customers experience high expectations during the sales cycle, but frequently hit a "trough of disillusionment" if onboarding drags out and the product proves difficult to use. To combat this, the First Value play is designed to guide the customer to a minimum viable win. First Value is about providing the right amount of knowledge to achieve a goal that truly matters to them, no more and no less. The success of this play relies on capturing both the "Heart" and the "Smart":

  • The Heart: This is the emotional core of the customer's experience. When you listen to their needs and guide them to an early accomplishment, they feel seen, heard, and valued. Without this early sense of accomplishment, customers are highly likely to disengage.
  • The Smart: This represents the successful adoption of your product. First Value is the psychological catalyst for the iterative loop. When a customer experiences solving their very first problem, overcoming a challenge, or creating a new capability, they are immediately more likely to come back to the product and try something else. It proves that the "minimum viable win" isn't just about checking a box; it is the critical first step in increasing their sophistication of use over time. When a customer achieves a fast win, that positive experience primes them for long-term product adoption and makes your solution a sticky part of their daily life.

2. Design & Build: Rightsizing First Value

When designing your First Value Play, the most critical rule is time: First Value should ideally be achieved within a couple of weeks. If your current implementation takes months, you must right-size the goal.

To refine and rightsize First Value, modern teams use a step-by-step reverse-engineering process:

  • Identify the larger objective the customer wants to achieve.
  • Ask yourself: Can they successfully accomplish this within two weeks?
  • If the answer is yes, you have found your First Value.
  • If the answer is no, ask: What smaller action must happen before they can accomplish that goal?
  • Continue this exercise of identifying smaller predicating steps until you land on an outcome that can be achieved within the two-week timeframe.

For example, if a customer bought a community platform to generate five warm leads a month, they cannot achieve that in two weeks. However, they can optimize their user profile to attract those leads within two weeks. That optimized profile becomes their First Value.

As you design this play, remember that the 80/20 rule will likely apply. Roughly 80% of your customers will pursue one of a few common First Value goals, meaning your team can build a predictable, repeatable process without reinventing the wheel for every account. Furthermore, you as the company have the power to completely redefine what "Go Live" means in order to support this rapid First Value approach.

3. Best Practices: Celebration and Gauging Health

Once the customer crosses the finish line and achieves their minimum viable win, you must put the final best practices of the play into action.

Customer and Internal Celebrations Because First Value is achieved so quickly after the Purchase & Welcome celebration, this celebration should be kept simple. An upbeat, visually distinct email, a personalized video, or a simple "have a coffee on me" gift card perfectly reinforces their feeling of successful progress. It is also highly recommended to use this moment to set up their next goal and potentially send an NPS survey to validate your onboarding approach.

Simultaneously, you must celebrate this milestone internally. Communicating early customer wins across the organization reminds everyone that CX is a whole-company effort, proving that non-financial wins today lay the foundation for revenue growth tomorrow.

First Value as the Canary in the Coal Mine Finally, tracking whether a customer achieves (or fails to achieve) First Value provides your earliest and most accurate data regarding customer health. First Value acts as a "canary in the coal mine" to determine account risk.

If a customer struggles here, it allows you to instantly evaluate:

  • Alignment: Are your product capabilities and the customer's desired use case truly aligned?
  • The Five Customer Needs (Skillset & Knowledge): Calling back to the Five Universal Customer Needs we covered previously, is your customer ripe for success? Do they have the tactical skillset required to execute effectively, or the knowledge to make critical decisions with the information available? Failing to reach First Value quickly often exposes a gap in these foundational needs.
  • Risk: What is missing from the relationship? Is there a lack of executive buy-in, enthusiasm, or understanding?

By flawlessly executing the First Value play, you cure the "trough of disillusionment" and launch your customer directly into the ongoing engagement loop of the "messy middle".
