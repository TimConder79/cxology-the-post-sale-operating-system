The 3 Essentials of Customer Success: Relevance, Value, and Trust

I've spent a lot of years trying to understand what makes customers stay.

I've seen companies with great products lose customers.

I've seen talented Customer Success teams work incredibly hard and still struggle with retention.

And I've seen companies create extraordinary customer relationships without doing anything particularly complicated.

Over time, I kept coming back to three things.

Relevance.

Value Achievement.

Trust.

They sound simple.

But I've come to believe that much of what we do in the post-sale either strengthens or weakens one of these three things.

The challenge isn't understanding that they matter.

It's designing a customer experience that consistently produces them.

1. Relevance: Does the customer still need what we're providing?

Relevance begins with understanding why the customer purchased.

Not simply what product they bought.

Why they bought it.

What problem were they trying to solve?

What needed to change?

What outcome were they hoping to achieve?

Who was involved in the decision?

What expectations were established?

Those answers are incredibly important because they give the post-sale organization a starting point.

And yet, I've seen plenty of companies lose that context almost immediately after the sale.

The deal closes.

The customer gets handed to Implementation or Customer Success.

A new team starts asking questions the customer already answered during the sales process.

Sometimes the customer has to explain all over again why they bought.

That's not a great way to begin a relationship.

Relevance begins before the handoff

One of the simplest things I've implemented is a structured transfer of knowledge from Sales to the post-sale team.

Four questions:

  • Why did the customer purchase?
  • Who are they, and who are the important people involved?
  • What was promised, and what wasn't included?
  • What does success mean to them?

Those answers give the post-sale team the context needed to begin helping the customer make progress.

But relevance doesn't stop there.

The reason someone purchased twelve months ago may not be the most important thing to them today.

Their business changes.

Leadership changes.

Priorities shift.

New challenges emerge.

Sometimes the original problem gets solved, and the customer needs to move on to something else.

If we keep delivering the experience we designed at purchase without revisiting what matters, we can become less relevant even while executing our original plan perfectly.

That's why I believe Ongoing Alignment is one of the most important moments in the customer journey.

We have to keep asking what matters now.

And we have to be willing to adjust the relationship accordingly.

Relevance isn't something you establish once. It's something you have to continually earn.

2. Value Achievement: Is the customer accomplishing what they came to do?

Being relevant isn't enough.

A customer can have an important problem that your product is well positioned to solve and still fail to achieve the outcome they purchased.

That's where Value Achievement comes in.

And it's different from activity.

A customer can attend every meeting.

Complete onboarding.

Finish training.

Log into the product regularly.

And still not accomplish what they came to do.

I've seen this happen.

The vendor believes the relationship is going well because all the expected activities are happening.

But the customer isn't getting closer to their goal.

Eventually, that disconnect becomes a retention problem.

Start by creating evidence early

One of the most important moments in the customer journey is First Value.

The customer needs an early indication that the decision they made is beginning to work.

It doesn't have to be the complete business outcome.

For some customers, that outcome may take months.

But there should be something meaningful they can accomplish much earlier.

A problem solved.

A process improved.

A useful result.

Something that lets the customer say:

This is starting to work.

That first success begins turning the promise made during the sales process into evidence.

But First Value is only the beginning.

Value needs to keep progressing

After First Value, the relationship enters what I often call the messy middle.

The customer is using the product.

They're trying to change how they work.

They're pursuing goals while managing competing priorities.

Some things go well.

Others don't.

This is where many Customer Success teams become reactive.

They check in.

They monitor usage.

They respond to problems.

But the customer still needs help making progress.

That's why I use Value Blocks.

A Value Block is a smaller, meaningful step toward a larger customer objective.

Instead of waiting months to see whether the customer achieved a major goal, we create opportunities for progress along the way.

Each step gives us something to work toward.

Something to measure.

Something to celebrate.

And something to learn from when it doesn't happen.

Over time, those moments of progress help the customer become more capable, more confident, and more sophisticated in how they use the product.

Value Achievement isn't a single milestone. It's an ongoing process of helping customers accomplish what matters to them.

3. Trust: Does the customer believe they can count on us?

Trust is often described as a relationship skill.

Build rapport.

Communicate frequently.

Be transparent.

Follow through.

Those things matter.

But I've come to think about trust a little differently.

Trust is the evidence a customer accumulates about whether they can count on your company.

Did the experience match what they were promised?

Did the team understand their goals?

Did commitments get kept?

Did they achieve something meaningful?

When something went wrong, did the company take responsibility?

Did the relationship adapt when their priorities changed?

Every one of those experiences gives the customer evidence.

Over time, that evidence either strengthens or weakens trust.

Trust is bigger than the CSM relationship

I've worked with plenty of excellent CSMs.

People who genuinely care about their customers and will do almost anything to help them succeed.

But even the best CSM can't compensate indefinitely for a company that repeatedly breaks trust elsewhere.

Sales creates expectations.

Implementation makes commitments.

Product determines what customers can accomplish.

Support shapes how problems get resolved.

Finance influences the commercial experience.

Leadership influences how the company responds when something important goes wrong.

The customer doesn't experience those departments separately.

They experience one company.

And every interaction contributes to their confidence in the relationship.

That's why trust can't be the responsibility of Customer Success alone.

It has to be supported by how the entire organization operates.

Progress creates evidence, and evidence builds trust

There's also a connection between trust and Value Achievement that I think is easy to overlook.

When a customer achieves First Value, they gain evidence that the purchase was a good decision.

When they accomplish a meaningful goal, they gain more.

When a new process becomes part of how their organization works, they gain more.

When the company helps them navigate a difficult situation, they gain more.

And when those results are connected back to the reason they purchased, the customer can see why continuing the relationship makes sense.

Progression creates evidence. Evidence builds trust.

That's much more durable than simply having a good relationship with the person managing the account.

These three elements reinforce one another

The interesting thing about Relevance, Value Achievement, and Trust is that they aren't independent.

They're connected.

Relevance tells us what matters to the customer.

Value Achievement demonstrates that we're helping them accomplish it.

Trust develops as the customer accumulates evidence that they can count on us.

And that trust creates opportunities for deeper conversations about what matters next.

Those conversations help us maintain relevance.

Which creates new opportunities to deliver value.

Which strengthens trust.

It's a cycle.

But it can also work in reverse.

We lose touch with the customer's changing priorities.

Our work becomes less relevant.

The customer stops making meaningful progress.

They have less evidence that the relationship is producing value.

Trust begins to erode.

And eventually, renewal becomes a difficult conversation.

The problem may appear at renewal, but it started much earlier.

The challenge is making these three things operational

When I originally wrote about these three elements, I thought of them primarily as principles for Strategic Customer Success.

I still believe they're useful principles.

But I've become increasingly convinced that principles aren't enough.

Telling a CSM to remain relevant, deliver value, and build trust doesn't explain what they should do on Tuesday.

It doesn't tell them what needs to happen next.

It doesn't tell leadership whether the customer is progressing.

And it doesn't tell the organization what to do when expected progress doesn't occur.

That's where the Post-Sale Operating System comes in.

We can design the important moments in the customer journey.

Define the plays that help customers progress through them.

Establish what First Value should look like.

Create a cadence of Value Blocks that move customers toward their goals.

Use Alignment Meetings to keep the relationship relevant.

Make customer progression visible.

And connect that progression to the revenue we're trying to retain and grow.

Now Relevance, Value Achievement, and Trust aren't simply things we hope our people create.

They're outcomes we intentionally design the customer experience to support.

What would you see across your customer base?

Imagine looking across your existing customers and being able to answer:

Do we understand what matters to each customer today?

Are they making meaningful progress toward those goals?

What evidence have they accumulated that the relationship is working?

Where has progression stalled?

What needs to happen next?

And what does all of that mean for the revenue connected to those customers?

Those are much more useful questions than simply asking whether customers are happy.

Or whether they're using the product.

Or whether their health scores are green.

Because retention isn't created by any one of those things.

It's shaped by what happens throughout the relationship.

Relevance keeps us connected to what matters.

Value Achievement gives the customer a reason to continue.

Trust gives them confidence that we can keep delivering.

The three essentials haven't changed.

What has changed is my belief that we need a system designed to produce them.
