Chapter 9

Play 1 — The Purchase & Welcome Play

The moment a customer says yes, a clock starts ticking. Don't let silence fill the space.

The moment a customer says "yes" to your product, a clock starts ticking. The transition from a prospect evaluating a solution to a new customer preparing for implementation is a critical juncture in the relationship. This is where your post-sale pipeline officially begins, and it starts with the Purchase & Welcome Play.

1. The Why: Mitigating Buyer’s Remorse and Building Loyalty

The immediate aftermath of a purchase decision is a vulnerable moment for new customers. Once the excitement of the sales cycle fades, it is entirely natural for customers to experience a moment of buyer’s remorse, sitting back and wondering, “Did I make the right decision?”.

A highly orchestrated Purchase & Welcome Play reinforces the customer's purchase decision and launches the relationship for success. It operates between two distinct "bookends": it begins the moment the customer makes the decision to purchase—often before the ink is even dry on the contract or money changes hands—and it ends with the official Kickoff meeting.

Because inflection points (moments that either build or block loyalty) often happen when you are not actively engaging with your customer, the Welcome phase is critical. If you leave the customer in silence while you handle internal administrative tasks, you are allowing them to fill in the blanks with their own anxiety. A well-designed play proactively eliminates loyalty blockers—such as a lack of clarity on what happens next or a significant lead time before kickoff—and replaces them with loyalty builders that educate the customer and clearly communicate roles and next steps.

2. Design & Build: Mapping the Play

When designing your Purchase & Welcome Play, there are three core components you must weave into the customer experience: Personalization, Celebration, and Setting Expectations.

The Internal Knowledge Transfer (IKT) Before you can personalize the customer's experience, you must master the internal handoff. As we established in Chapter 2, Lincoln Murphy famously stated that customers hate nothing more than having to answer the same questions they just spent months explaining to Sales.

To build a smooth IKT into your play, your Sales team must answer just four key questions before the CS team takes over:

  • Why? Why did they purchase?
  • Who? Who are they as a company, and who are the key people we need to know?
  • What? What was promised, what was sold, and what wasn't sold?
  • Success? What does success mean for them?. Capturing this in a "Single Source of Truth" (like your CRM) ensures your CS team walks into the relationship looking like informed, strategic partners.

Automated and Curated Touchpoints To design the flow of this play, modern teams map out specific triggers and actions. For example:

  • The Trigger: Sales updates the opportunity to "Closed-Won," triggering an automated internal alert.
  • Automated Email #1: Even before the contract is fully processed, an automated email goes to the customer expressing enthusiasm for the partnership.
  • Curated Touchpoint: The VP of CS assigns a CSM, who reviews the IKT data and records a short, personalized 20-30 second video welcoming the customer by name and referencing their specific business goals.
  • Action Step: The CSM sends the video along with an email clearly setting expectations for the upcoming Kickoff meeting.

The Psychology of Celebration Celebration is a critical step in the Welcome Play. Robert Cialdini, author of Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, notes that humans are wired with a psychological trigger: when someone gives you something, you feel an obligation to repay it in kind. By sending a welcome celebration—whether it’s a physical gift, a customized video, or a thoughtful digital resource—you anchor a tiny bit of positive obligation that makes it psychologically harder for the customer to second-guess their buying decision.

Don't forget internal celebration, either! Ringing a sales gong or adding a marble to a "new customer jar" boosts company culture and reminds the entire organization that they are part of the winning customer experience.

3. Best Practices: Execution at Scale

To make sure this play doesn't collapse under its own weight, you must implement strict best practices.

  • Action Must Overcome Distraction: You need an automated, immediate trigger to launch this play. You must "stupid-proof" the first step so that it occurs every single time, even if your top sales rep hits the lottery and never comes back to work.
  • Balance Customization and Consistency: Personalization is great, but it takes time. You must find the "Goldilocks balance"—not too big, not too small—where your team builds true relationships without getting bogged down in administrative tasks. Use templates to create efficiency and eliminate excuses, but leave dedicated blanks within those templates for CSMs to inject their own personality and relationship-building details.
  • Eliminate Local Copies: Have one link to the official version of your email templates and play documents. Eliminate local copies saved on individual hard drives so that if leadership updates the play, the whole team immediately uses the current version.
  • Track and Measure: Decide what metrics matter for this play. Best practices include tracking IKT completion rates, whether non-automated emails are sent within a specific number of hours, and the total time elapsed between the purchase and the Kickoff meeting.

By launching your Welcome Play with intention, you replace post-sale anxiety with immediate trust, perfectly teeing up the next crucial inflection point.

From Philosophy to Execution

Up to this point, the Purchase & Welcome Play has been framed as a customer experience principle. It exists because the moment immediately after purchase is fragile. A customer has made a commitment, but the value has not arrived yet. The sales process has created belief, but the post-sale experience has not yet proven that belief was well placed.

This is where many companies unintentionally lose momentum. They are not ignoring the customer. They are processing the deal, updating systems, assigning ownership, preparing implementation, and gathering information. Internally, work is happening. Externally, the customer may experience silence.

That silence is the problem.

The Purchase & Welcome Play exists to make sure the customer feels momentum while the company coordinates behind the scenes.

At some point, a play has to stop being an idea and become something the team can actually run. The difference between a company that talks about customer experience and a company that consistently delivers it is not intention. It is execution. The play is not just the belief that customers should feel welcomed. It is a sequence of actions, signals, and handoffs that happen every time, whether you are watching or not.

The Play in Motion

The Purchase & Welcome Play unfolds across a short window of time, but the impact is larger than the timeframe suggests. The customer experiences a simple progression: they make the decision to buy, they receive immediate reinforcement that the decision matters, they are introduced to what happens next, and they arrive at kickoff with clarity instead of uncertainty.

Behind that simple customer experience is a coordinated internal sequence. Sales triggers the play. Systems send the first signal. Internal knowledge transfer carries the story forward. Customer Success enters with context. The kickoff is scheduled with the right people, the right expectations, and the right preparation.

The point of the visual is not to make the process look more sophisticated than it is. The point is to make the process visible enough that it can be repeated. If the team cannot see the sequence, they cannot improve the sequence. If ownership is unclear, the welcome depends on memory and goodwill. If timing is undefined, the customer waits while the company gets organized.

What Powers This Play

A play becomes operational when the supporting assets are clear. Without them, the welcome depends on individual effort. With them, the team has a shared way to act, adapt, and improve.

Internal Knowledge Transfer Template

The IKT captures why the customer purchased, who matters, what was promised, what was not promised, and what success means in the customer’s world.

Welcome Communication

The first customer-facing message creates the early signal: enthusiasm, clarity, appreciation, and confidence that the next step is already in motion.

CSM Introduction

The first human touch from Customer Success should not sound generic. It should show that the team listened during the buying process and is prepared to continue from there.

Kickoff Scheduling Framework

The kickoff request should make action easy by clarifying who should attend, what to prepare, what will be covered, and how to schedule the meeting.

These assets do not need to be complicated. In fact, they should not be. The best play assets are simple enough for the team to use consistently and specific enough to prevent the experience from becoming generic. They create the right amount of structure so that the team can focus its judgment where it matters.

Templates create consistency. Consistency creates trust. Trust creates momentum.

Run This Play with AI

AI makes this play more practical because it reduces the friction between information and action. The goal is not to outsource judgment. The goal is to help the team move faster from what they know about the customer to what the customer needs to hear next.

The inputs still matter. A weak internal handoff will produce weak outputs. But when the IKT is strong, AI can quickly turn that context into a welcome email, a short CSM video script, a kickoff scheduling note, or a first-touch plan that feels specific to the customer instead of copied from a template library.

This is where the Purchase & Welcome Play becomes more than a static process. The play provides the structure. The IKT provides the context. AI helps convert that context into usable execution. The CSM still owns the relationship, but the administrative burden of starting well becomes much lighter.

AI does not replace judgment. It accelerates execution so your team can focus on the moments that matter.

Where This Leads

The Purchase & Welcome Play is not about making a good first impression once. It is about designing a system that creates the right first impression every time.

When this play works, the customer does not enter kickoff wondering what happens next. They arrive with clarity. They know who they are working with. They understand the immediate path forward. They feel that the company has been paying attention. Most importantly, they have not been left alone in the most vulnerable moment after purchase.

That is why this play matters. It replaces anxiety with trust, silence with motion, and a handoff with continuity. It prepares the customer for the kickoff, and it prepares the company to deliver on the promise that was just sold.
