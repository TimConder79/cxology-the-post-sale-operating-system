Glossary & Index

The concepts this book introduces, what each one means, and every chapter where it shows up.

These are the concepts this book introduces or uses in a specific way. Each entry gives a short definition and links to every chapter where the term appears. Selecting a chapter takes you to the first place the term is used there and highlights it; the number shows how many times it appears in that chapter.

ABCDEFGHILMNOPRSVY

A

Advocate

The third stage (60%), answering "Can someone inside the customer carry the case forward without us?" A credible internal advocate has the evidence and language to carry the business case when you are not in the room. The opportunity advances when that advocacy produces an explicit commitment.

Appears inPart II intro (3)Ch 5 (2)Ch 6 (18)Ch 7 (6)Ch 8Ch 13Ch 14Ch 15 (2)Ch 19Ch 21

Align

The second stage (40%), answering "Is it relevant to something the customer is trying to accomplish?" The opportunity is connected to a customer need, goal, or reason for purchase. It advances when the customer agrees with the business case and someone inside their organization can carry it.

Appears inPart II intro (4)Ch 4Ch 5 (14)Ch 6 (3)Ch 8Part III introCh 12 (2)Ch 13 (3)Ch 14Ch 15 (2)Ch 19Ch 21

Align / Engage / Achieve

The three phases of the customer lifecycle. Align happens once (Purchase & Welcome through First Value). Engage is a repeating loop of Value Blocks, Sharing Insights, and Alignment Meetings. Achieve is renewal and growth, after which the customer returns to the Engage loop. This Align is a lifecycle phase, not the Align stage of the post-sale pipeline.

Appears inPart III intro (2)

Alignment Meeting

The recurring meeting that brings the relationship back to the reason it began, checks what has changed, and sets the next objective. It replaces the QBR's backward-looking report with forward-looking alignment.

Appears inIntroductionCh 1Part II intro (4)Ch 5Ch 6 (5)Ch 7 (2)Ch 8 (2)Part III intro (6)Ch 10Ch 13 (5)Ch 14 (10)Ch 15 (18)Ch 16 (9)Ch 18 (2)Ch 19 (2)Ch 20 (2)Ch 21 (3)Ch 22 (6)Conclusion (2)

Anchor meeting

The most recent kickoff or Alignment Meeting, used as the reference point for measuring progress and change.

Appears inCh 15

Availability

The percentage of a person's capacity realistically available for direct customer work after indirect and unplanned effort are accounted for. CXology uses 65 percent as a planning starting point to validate, not a universal benchmark.

Appears inCh 22 (6)

Avoidable churn / unavoidable churn

Unavoidable churn comes from events outside the post-sale team's influence, such as an acquisition, a shutdown, a budget freeze, or a customer outgrowing the solution. Avoidable churn is the churn your company could have prevented, and it is what the operating system is designed to reduce.

Appears inCh 1 (6)Ch 4Ch 17 (3)Ch 20 (2)

B

Big Bang implementation

Launching the full product, all capabilities, and all users at once. The book recommends an iterative approach instead, with First Value as the first cycle.

Appears inCh 11 (3)

C

Canonical play

The standard, designed version of a play that every team member runs, so the customer experience depends on the system rather than on who owns the account.

Appears inPart III introCh 9 (2)Ch 10 (2)Ch 11 (2)Ch 12 (2)Ch 13 (3)Ch 14 (2)Ch 15 (2)Ch 16 (2)

CS maturity: reactive, proactive, strategic

The three stages post-sale organizations move through. Reactive teams fight fires and are surprised by churn. Proactive teams anticipate risk. Strategic teams govern the post-sale motion with the same rigor as the sales pipeline.

Appears inCh 1 (3)

D

Designed effort

The full effort required to deliver a play or service moment well: preparation, execution, and follow-through. A 60-minute meeting that needs 30 minutes of preparation and 20 minutes of follow-through has a designed effort of 110 minutes. It is the bridge from journey design to capacity planning.

Appears inPart IV introCh 22 (11)

Dual output

What a play produces when it works. It helps the customer progress toward the outcome they care about, and the evidence of that progress can count toward an opportunity's next pipeline stage when it meets that stage's exit criteria. Completing the play does neither on its own.

Appears inPart III intro

E

Effort mix

How a team's time actually splits: direct effort (designed proactive work and reactive customer work), indirect effort (necessary internal work), and unplanned effort (unexpected or avoidable work that consumes productive capacity).

Appears inCh 22 (5)

Evidence layer

The living record of value created across the relationship, from the reason for purchase through First Value, Value Blocks, insights, and Alignment Meetings, including what has not worked.

Appears inCh 14Ch 16 (2)

Execution layer

AI's role in the operating system: helping the team execute the designed plays consistently, rather than serving as a content generator. AI can produce the output; humans own the outcome.

Appears inIntroductionCh 3Part IV intro (2)Ch 18 (12)Conclusion (2)

Exit criteria

The observable evidence an opportunity must show to leave a pipeline stage. The stage reflects what you can prove, so an opportunity also moves back when the evidence behind its stage no longer holds.

Appears inPart II intro (3)Ch 4Ch 5Ch 6Ch 7Ch 8 (2)Part III introConclusion (4)

F

First Use

The moment the customer starts using the solution on their own. It is the first step toward First Value, not the end of onboarding support.

Appears inCh 11 (25)

First Value

The first moment the customer feels they have achieved something meaningful from their purchase, ideally within a few weeks of kickoff. It belongs to the customer, not to your implementation plan. For a first-term renewal, First Value is the evidence that moves it from Identify to Align.

Appears inIntroduction (3)Part II intro (4)Ch 4 (9)Ch 5Ch 8 (2)Part III intro (5)Ch 10 (25)Ch 11 (39)Ch 12 (55)Ch 13 (17)Ch 14 (3)Ch 15 (4)Ch 16 (5)Ch 18Ch 20 (7)Ch 21 (8)Ch 22 (2)Conclusion (3)

Five customer needs

The diagnostic layer behind Value Blocks: skill set, knowledge, cost (time, effort, money), competing demands, and the key contact's ability to drive change.

Appears inCh 13 (4)

Five Questions

The kickoff questions that define success with the customer: what one thing must go right, how they define success, what your role is, what could put the effort at risk, and what would exceed their wildest dreams. The answers become the baseline for every Alignment Meeting.

Appears inCh 10 (12)Ch 11Ch 12Ch 15 (4)Ch 19

Five-part insight formula

The structure for writing an insight: a one-line gist, the background, the results and how they could be different, the action to take, and a closing reinforcement.

Appears inCh 14 (2)

The floor

The renewal opportunity: it exists from day one, with a known close date at contract end and a value equal to the current ARR up for renewal. The lifecycle plays do most of the work that supports it, but it advances on the same evidence standard as any other opportunity.

Appears inPart II intro (4)Ch 9 (2)Ch 16 (2)

Follow-through

Keeping the process moving after a meeting by doing what you committed to and confirming what the customer committed to. Distinct from follow-up, which only checks in.

Appears inCh 10 (13)Ch 11 (7)Ch 15Ch 17Part IV introCh 22 (5)Conclusion (2)

G

Go Live

The company's milestone that the solution is ready. The book separates it from First Value and encourages companies to define Go Live so it does not delay the customer's first win.

Appears inIntroductionCh 12 (12)Ch 20

The growth

Expansion opportunities. They enter the pipeline at Identify once they are specific enough to manage, and each moves on its own evidence, so it can sit in a different stage than the renewal.

Appears inPart II intro (2)Ch 16 (2)

H

Heart and smart

The two dimensions of First Value. Heart is the customer's feeling of accomplishment. Smart is the adoption logic underneath it that makes future use more likely.

Appears inCh 12

I

Identify

The first pipeline stage (20%), answering "Is there a real revenue opportunity?" The renewal enters here at purchase, and expansion opportunities enter once they are specific enough to manage. A first-term renewal stays here until the customer reaches First Value, unless a documented exception sets a revised First Value date. Other opportunities advance when they are tied to a customer need or goal.

Appears inPart II intro (5)Ch 4 (18)Ch 5 (2)Ch 6Ch 9 (2)Ch 10 (2)Ch 11 (3)Ch 12 (2)Ch 13Ch 14Ch 15 (2)

Inflection point

A moment in the customer journey where meaningful progression is expected, and where you have an opportunity to either build or block loyalty. Many happen when you are not in the room, and recognizing them is your responsibility, not the customer's.

Appears inIntroduction (2)Ch 4Ch 8Part III intro (6)Ch 9Ch 11Ch 12Ch 14Ch 15 (3)Part IV introCh 18Ch 19Ch 21 (7)Ch 22 (2)Conclusion (3)

Intent

The fourth stage (90%), answering "Has the customer committed to moving forward?" The customer has given a verbal yes or its equivalent, with a credible path forward: timing, ownership, approvals, and next steps. Intent is not a signed contract.

Appears inPart II intro (4)Ch 6 (4)Ch 7 (20)Ch 8 (2)Ch 15Ch 16Ch 19 (3)Ch 21 (3)

Internal Knowledge Transfer (IKT)

The handoff from Sales to the post-sale team, built on four questions: why the customer bought, who they are and who matters, what was promised (and what was not), and what success means to them.

Appears inCh 9 (13)Ch 10 (2)Ch 18 (2)Ch 19 (5)

L

Lifecycle play

A designed motion for a specific inflection point in the customer journey, with a trigger, a sequence of actions, supporting assets, an intended outcome, and a way to measure whether it worked. The book covers eight lifecycle plays, from Purchase & Welcome to Renew & Grow.

Appears inPart II intro (2)Ch 8Part III introCh 10Ch 13Ch 14Ch 15Ch 17Ch 18 (2)Ch 20 (2)

Loyalty blocker

A barrier between your offering and the value the customer wants. Occasionally a deal breaker, but far more often a small annoyance that adds up over time.

Appears inCh 4Part III introCh 9Ch 10 (3)Ch 11 (6)Ch 13

Loyalty builder

Either a best practice someone on your team already uses, or a response you design to counter a specific loyalty blocker.

Appears inPart III introCh 14

M

Marble jar

A ritual that makes the accumulation of customer wins visible inside the company, whether a literal jar, a win report, or a shared story, so retention and expansion are celebrated rather than invisible.

Appears inCh 8

Messy middle

The long stretch between First Value and renewal, where most post-sale relationships lose momentum. It is navigated by three plays working together: Value Blocks, Sharing Insights, and Alignment Meetings.

Appears inPart III intro (2)Ch 13 (5)Ch 14 (3)Ch 15Ch 17

Mid-Year Reframe

A deliberate renewal-readiness moment around the midpoint of the contract to re-anchor value, surface risk and timing, and spot growth signals while there is still time to act.

Appears inCh 16 (3)

Minimum viable win

The smallest meaningful win that proves progress against the customer's reason for buying. Not all the marbles; the first important marble.

Appears inCh 12

N

Net Revenue Close

The final stage (100%), answering "Did the revenue outcome actually close?" The commercial process is complete and the outcome is final, whether a full renewal, a reduced one, an expansion, or a loss. It is not the end of the relationship: the next cycle of goals and opportunities begins.

Appears inPart II intro (5)Ch 7 (4)Ch 8 (8)Ch 16Ch 19Ch 21Conclusion

Next-best-action

AI that recommends what to do about a signal: which play to run, which contact to reach, and what message fits the moment. The CSM still decides.

Appears inCh 18

O

Onboarding motions

The six motions every onboarding moves through: Align, Configure, Connect, Verify, Transform, and First Use. The form varies by company; the logic does not.

Appears inCh 11 (15)Ch 22

One Data Spine

The single, trusted customer record the whole operating system runs on, built by centralizing the data, democratizing access, and eliminating local copies.

Appears inPart IV intro (2)Ch 18Ch 19 (12)Ch 21Conclusion (2)

P

Post-Sale Operating System

A Post-Sale Operating System is the integrated management system a company uses to design customer progression, execute the work intended to support it, observe whether that progression occurred, and translate the evidence into decisions about customer health, capacity, retention, expansion, and revenue forecasting. In plain terms, it connects what the customer needs to accomplish, what the company does to help, what actually changes, and what that means for the revenue.

Appears inPreface (2)IntroductionCh 1 (4)Ch 6Ch 8Part III introCh 10 (2)Ch 11Ch 12Ch 13Ch 14 (2)Ch 16Ch 17Ch 18 (2)Ch 19 (2)Ch 20Ch 21 (3)Ch 22Conclusion

Post-sale pipeline

A Post-Sale Pipeline is the structured revenue process for managing existing-customer retention and growth opportunities from identification through customer commitment and Net Revenue Close. Revenue opportunities, not customers, move through five stages: Identify, Align, Advocate, Intent, and Net Revenue Close, and each advances only when observable evidence satisfies the exit criteria for the next stage.

Post-sale unification

Running the post-sale motion as one system across every team that touches the customer, instead of a collection of functions. Customers experience one journey, not your org chart, and every seam between teams either builds confidence or creates friction.

Appears inCh 2Ch 21

Progression-based health score

A health score that asks whether the customer is progressing through the system the way a healthy customer should, rather than only whether they are at risk.

Appears inIntroduction (3)Ch 1Ch 2Ch 3Part IV introCh 18 (2)Ch 19 (5)Ch 20 (22)Ch 21

R

Reset

A separate kind of meeting for a relationship that has become unhealthy or where terms need to be redefined. Alignment keeps a healthy relationship moving; a reset is needed when it has already broken rhythm.

Appears inCh 4Ch 15 (3)Ch 16 (3)

Revenue opportunity

The unit that moves through the post-sale pipeline. Every customer creates at least one retention opportunity at purchase, and expansion opportunities are added when they are specific enough to manage, so one customer can have several opportunities in different stages at once.

Appears inPart II introCh 20Conclusion (2)

Right-touch execution

Matching the level and type of engagement to what each customer actually needs, somewhere between high-touch for everyone and fully digital. AI and automation make it attainable at scale when they sit inside a coherent operating model.

Appears inCh 1 (2)

S

Stage probability weights

The default forecast probabilities for each pipeline stage: Identify 20%, Align 40%, Advocate 60%, Intent 90%, Net Revenue Close 100%. They are standardized assumptions tied to increasing levels of commercial evidence, not measured close rates, and they apply the same way across segments. Opportunity value × stage probability = weighted forecast value.

Appears inPart II intro (2)Ch 4Part III introCh 12Part IV introCh 19

Supporting plays

Plays that protect the lifecycle when the relationship changes shape: Key Contact Change, when a champion or sponsor leaves, and Offboarding, when a customer decides to leave.

Appears inPart III introCh 16Ch 17 (4)Conclusion

V

Value Block

A small, purposeful step toward a larger customer objective, sized to be completed by the next Alignment Meeting. Originally called Goal Blocks.

Appears inCh 1Part II intro (2)Ch 8Part III intro (6)Ch 10Ch 12 (2)Ch 13 (44)Ch 14 (9)Ch 15 (11)Ch 16 (9)Ch 18 (2)Ch 19Ch 20Ch 21 (4)Ch 22 (2)Conclusion

Value Block Library

The reusable collection of common Value Blocks, each tied to a customer need, with its resources and completion criteria.

Appears inCh 13 (4)

Y

You-We-Me framework

The structure for the Key Contact Change meeting: start with the new contact's goals (You), then the shared work (We), and only then your company (Me).

Appears inCh 17 (2)
