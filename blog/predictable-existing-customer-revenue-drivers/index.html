The 5 Drivers of Predictable Existing-Customer Revenue

Predictable existing-customer revenue doesn’t start with a better forecast.

It starts much earlier.

A renewal or expansion is the result of a series of things happening before the revenue ever arrives. Customers make progress. They realize value. Their goals stay aligned. Their confidence grows. Eventually, they make another buying decision.

If we want the revenue at the end of that process to become more predictable, we need to manage what creates it.

I’ve come to think about that as a chain:

Visibility → Progression → Predictability → Execution → Economics

Each link answers a different question.

What revenue are we trying to manage?

What needs to happen for the customer to move forward?

What evidence tells us where that revenue is headed?

What work creates that progress?

And can we actually deliver that work consistently?

That chain is at the heart of the Post-Sale Operating System.

Predictable existing-customer revenue comes from making revenue visible, defining the customer progression behind it, using evidence to improve the forecast, executing the work that creates progress, and funding enough capacity to deliver that work consistently.

Visibility: Start by Making the Revenue Visible

Before we can manage existing-customer revenue, we have to be able to see it.

Think about the visibility we have before the sale.

An opportunity enters the pipeline. It moves through defined stages. Evidence increases our confidence that the opportunity will close. Leadership can inspect it, challenge assumptions, and understand where future revenue is coming from.

After the sale, that visibility often changes.

We can see contract dates, usage, health scores, support cases, meetings, and dozens of other data points.

But those aren’t the same as seeing revenue progression.

If a meaningful portion of next year’s revenue depends on customers renewing or expanding, leadership should be able to see that revenue developing long before the final decision.

That’s the job of the Post-Sale Pipeline.

It makes existing-customer revenue visible.

But seeing the revenue raises another question:

What actually moves it forward?

Progression: Define What Moving Forward Means

Activity is easy to see.

Progression is harder.

A customer attended the QBR.

A CSM completed the onboarding tasks.

Usage increased.

An executive sponsor joined a meeting.

Those things might matter. But none of them automatically means the customer has progressed.

Progression asks a different question:

What is different now because of the work we’ve done together?

Has the customer reached First Value?

Are they accomplishing the goal that caused them to buy?

Has the product become part of how they operate?

Are the right people aligned around the value being created?

Is there evidence that they intend to continue or expand the relationship?

If we don’t define progression, we end up managing activity and hoping it produces the outcome we want.

Once progression is clear, something else becomes possible.

We can start making better predictions.

Predictability: Forecast the Evidence, Not the Hope

A renewal date tells us when a decision will happen.

It doesn’t tell us what that decision will be.

A health score can tell us something about the customer’s current condition.

It doesn’t necessarily tell us whether they have decided to renew.

Predictability comes from understanding the evidence that precedes the revenue decision.

That’s why a Post-Sale Pipeline matters.

Instead of treating renewal as something that suddenly becomes important 90 or 120 days before the contract ends, we can see the evidence building toward that decision over time.

The goal isn’t perfect forecasting.

It’s fewer surprises.

When progression becomes visible, leadership can inspect what’s happening, challenge assumptions, and intervene while there’s still time to change the outcome.

But that introduces another question.

If a customer isn’t progressing, what are we going to do about it?

Execution: Design the Work That Creates Progress

This may be the most important link in the chain.

Knowing where a customer is doesn’t move them forward.

Work does.

Throughout the customer lifecycle, there are moments that disproportionately influence what happens next.

The first meeting matters.

Onboarding decisions matter.

First Value matters.

Goal attainment matters.

Habit formation matters.

Ongoing alignment matters.

The renewal and growth decision matters.

We should design the work around those moments rather than asking every CSM to figure out what to do next for every customer.

That’s what plays are for.

A play defines what should happen at an important moment in the customer’s progression.

Then the operating question becomes much simpler:

Know what needs to happen next. Know whether it happened. Act when it doesn’t.

Now we’re no longer just observing the customer.

We’re managing the progression that creates the outcome.

But even a perfectly designed play is useless if nobody has the capacity to execute it.

Economics: Can We Actually Deliver the Experience We Designed?

This is where strategy meets reality.

We can define an incredible customer journey.

We can create thoughtful plays.

We can decide exactly what should happen at every critical moment.

And then assign a CSM more work than a human being can possibly accomplish.

The operating system breaks.

That’s why capacity planning belongs in the same conversation as retention and growth.

The question isn’t simply:

How much ARR can a CSM manage?

It’s:

How much customer progression can a CSM create?

Start with the work.

What needs to happen for customers to progress?

How much effort does that require?

Which work requires human judgment and relationships?

Which work can be standardized?

Where can technology and AI help?

What should we stop doing entirely?

Only then can we understand the capacity required to deliver the customer experience we’ve designed.

The Chain Matters More Than Any Individual Link

Visibility without progression gives us another dashboard.

Progression without execution gives us a journey map.

Execution without capacity creates exhausted teams and inconsistent customer experiences.

And forecasting without all of them gives us numbers that look precise without necessarily making the revenue more predictable.

The power comes from connecting them.

Visibility → Progression → Predictability → Execution → Economics

Make the revenue visible.

Understand the customer progression behind it.

Use evidence of that progression to improve predictability.

Design the work that creates progress.

Fund the capacity required to execute that work consistently.

That’s how existing-customer revenue becomes more predictable.

Not because we got better at predicting the outcome.

Because we got better at managing what creates it.

Retention is a designed outcome.
