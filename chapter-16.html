Chapter 16

Play 8 — The Renew & Grow Play

When the system is working, renewal is not a moment. It is the inevitable outcome of sustained value, visible progress, and aligned ambition.

The Renew & Grow Play is often misunderstood because most companies encounter it too late. They treat renewal as a contract motion, a forecast category, or a moment when the customer must be convinced to continue. Then the renewal carries the weight of everything the relationship did or did not accomplish. The team is suddenly asked to prove value, defend usage, explain price, find decision makers, navigate procurement, and position growth, all while the clock works against them.

That is not a renewal play. That is a rescue attempt.

In a Post-Sale Operating System, the Renew & Grow Play is not where value is created. It is where value is confirmed. The customer can look back and see a clear line from the reason they bought, to the first win, to the Value Blocks completed, the insights shared, and the Alignment Meetings that kept the work pointed at outcomes. When the earlier plays have done their job, renewal feels like the obvious continuation of work already underway.

Operating Principle

Renewal is not where value is proven. It is where value is confirmed.

The chapter title matters. It is not simply Renew. It is Renew & Grow. The goal is to help the customer make the next right investment based on the progress they have made. Sometimes that is a straightforward renewal. Sometimes it is expansion into more users, products, business units, or services. Sometimes it is a reset because the customer is not ready to grow but has enough evidence to continue. And sometimes the right outcome is an intentional exit handled with care. The play has to handle all of those paths without losing clarity or trust.

Auto-renewing contracts deserve a specific note. They feel like the easiest renewals in the book because no one has to ask for anything. The contract rolls over, the revenue stays in the forecast, and attention moves to the accounts that need a decision. But the customer is still deciding whether you are worth it; they just are not telling you. An auto-renewal that nobody paid attention to can become a surprise cancellation or downgrade the following year. That raises a harder question: do you remind the customer the renewal is coming, or let it roll over quietly?

I have been in these situations. When your company is winning, it is easy to say you should tell the customer. When you are not, it is easy to justify flying under the radar. That can be a tough choice depending on your circumstances, and there is no perfect answer. But if your executives cannot confront the brutal facts and improve, the outcome is nearly written in stone.

What the Customer Is Thinking
  • “Can I show my leadership this was worth it?”
  • “Did we get what we were promised?”
  • “Is this still the right fit for where we’re going?”
  • “What else could this solve for us?”

They rarely ask these out loud. The play is designed to answer them anyway.

Build the Canonical Renew & Grow Play

The canonical Renew & Grow Play begins with a simple belief: the renewal conversation should start long before the renewal date. Not to ask for a signature early, but because renewal is part of how the relationship operates, and mature teams name it before it becomes awkward. Shortly after kickoff, the CSM can ask a light, direct question: how do you want to manage the path toward renewal? Some customers want a formal conversation ninety days out. Others need a six-month checkpoint because of budget cycles, procurement, or legal review. The point is to remove the mystery.

That conversation also reveals how the customer actually buys, which is one of the most important lessons in this play. Customers do not renew only because they are happy. They renew when value, timing, budget, procurement, executive sponsorship, and internal priority all come together.

Commercial Reality

The biggest risk to renewal is not always dissatisfaction. Often, it is misalignment with how the customer budgets, buys, approves, and prioritizes.

Renewal is where three systems meet. The first is the value system: what the customer hoped to accomplish, what progress has been made, and what still matters. The second is the customer’s buying system: how budgets are set, who approves, how procurement and legal work, and whether a new executive or a vendor-consolidation mandate has changed the rules. The third is your commercial system: terms, pricing, timing, expansion paths, and the handoff between CS and Sales. A strong play brings these into alignment before they collide.

With software spend under more scrutiny than ever, a warm relationship is not enough. A champion can believe in the product and still lose the internal business case if usage is unclear or outcomes are vague. The play has to prepare the customer to defend the investment when you are not in the room. That is also why ninety days is a useful minimum, not a strategy. For some customers it is too late. Work backward from how the customer buys, not forward from the contract date.

None of this can be rushed at the end. Every Alignment Meeting, insight, and Value Block should add to the case, so the renewal is easier to approve because the case has already been built.

Map the Play in Motion

The Renew & Grow Play is a motion, not a meeting. It starts when renewal expectations are set early, builds as evidence accumulates through the earlier plays, sharpens at the midpoint of the contract, comes together as the team prepares the ask, and culminates in the Renewal & Growth conversation before resetting into the next cycle.

01 · Set the Path

Normalize renewal early, understand the customer’s timing, and make the path to continuation part of the relationship rather than a late-stage surprise.

02 · Build Evidence

Capture First Value, completed Value Blocks, shared insights, adoption patterns, stakeholder engagement, and progress against the customer’s stated goals.

03 · Reframe Mid-Year

Use the midpoint to re-anchor value, understand what has changed, surface risk, identify expansion signals, and align to budgeting or procurement realities.

04 · Prepare the Ask

Create the renewal readiness brief, confirm stakeholders, coordinate CS and Sales, prepare for objections, and define the most likely renewal or growth path.

05 · Renew & Reset

Run the Renewal & Growth conversation, confirm the commercial path, close the contract, celebrate the win, and translate the next goal into a new lifecycle plan.

01 · Set the Path

Setting the path is the early conversation described above: naming renewal shortly after kickoff, learning the customer’s budget and approval timing, and agreeing on when the renewal conversation will happen. It makes renewal a normal part of the relationship instead of a sudden commercial interruption.

02 · Build Evidence

Renewal depends on evidence because memory is too fragile. The people who decide on renewal often were not in the room for the strong moments. Procurement may not know the story. Finance may not see the operational value. A new executive may inherit the relationship and ask why the company is still paying.

The evidence layer is the living record of the relationship: the reason for purchase, the outcomes captured at kickoff, First Value, completed Value Blocks, insights delivered, adoption patterns, stakeholder engagement, and the changes discussed in Alignment Meetings. It should be qualitative and quantitative, and it needs interpretation. Low usage can mean risk, or a narrow but highly valuable use. Low seat utilization can mean danger, or a clean chance to right-size and rebuild trust.

The evidence should also include what has not worked. A renewal story with only wins sounds like marketing. One that includes progress, friction, what was learned, and a credible next plan sounds like partnership.

Value Defense

If the team has to build the case for renewal at the end, the system waited too long.

Strong evidence also builds the bridge to growth. If the customer has achieved the first outcome, the question becomes what progress should look like now. If the current package limits the next goal, or an adjacent team could benefit from what has been proven, growth comes through evidence instead of pressure.

03 · Reframe Mid-Year

The most overlooked moment in this play is the midpoint of the contract. The excitement of purchase has faded, the renewal date still feels far away, and the relationship can go operationally quiet while its strategic relevance erodes. The Mid-Year Reframe is a deliberate renewal-readiness moment, not a business review with a new name. It asks what has changed in the customer’s business, what has been achieved, what has stalled, who else needs to be involved, and what internal timing the team should understand now.

This is also where growth usually becomes visible. Expansion rarely appears fully formed at the end of a contract. It shows up as signals: a team using the product more deeply than expected, a new business unit showing interest, a request for a capability outside the original scope, a goal that has outgrown the current package. The reframe is a natural place to explore those signals without turning the conversation into a pitch.

The principle matters more than the calendar. Some customers need the reframe four months in because budgeting starts early. Multi-year agreements may need a more deliberate cadence. The system should require that a reframe happens and let the team judge when it will matter most.

04 · Prepare the Ask

As the renewal window approaches, the team assembles a clear point of view: the evidence, the stakeholders, usage and license fit, the customer’s buying process, and the most likely path. Is this a straightforward renewal? Does the customer need a value defense? Is there a right-sizing risk, or a real expansion case?

Growth needs discipline here. Not every customer should expand. The team should distinguish between customers who are ready to grow, customers who need to stabilize, and customers drifting away from the ideal fit. Growth that creates delivery strain or unmanaged expectations damages retention later.

CS and Sales also need to be coordinated before the customer conversation, not during it. CS brings the evidence, trust, and context. Sales or Account Management brings commercial discipline and deal execution. If expansion is likely, the commercial owner should be involved early enough to avoid a clumsy handoff. The customer should experience one team.

05 · Renew & Reset

The Renewal & Growth conversation should feel like a continuation of the Alignment Meeting, not a commercial ambush. Start with where the relationship began and what the customer hoped to accomplish. Move to what is working, what has changed, and what matters next. Only then move to the renewal and growth path. A renewal decision is a confidence decision before it is a financial one, and the conversation has to answer the confidence questions before the ask carries weight.

Then make the ask clearly. Many teams talk around renewal because they fear sounding commercial, and the avoidance creates confusion. If the relationship is healthy, say so and ask for the renewal. If expansion is warranted, connect it to the customer’s goals. If concerns remain, name them and agree on how they will be addressed.

Clear Ask

A mature renewal conversation does not hide the ask. It earns the right to make the ask by grounding it in value, evidence, and the customer’s next goal.

Finally, treat the signature as a reset, not an ending. Too many teams create urgency leading up to the signature and silence after it. Thank the customer, confirm what was agreed, document the next goal, schedule the next Alignment Meeting, and start shaping the next Value Blocks. Signing the renewal should open the next chapter.

Identify the Critical Assets

The Renew & Grow Play needs a few assets that make value visible, commercial risk manageable, and next-year planning concrete. They do not replace the customer conversation. They make it better.

Renewal Readiness Brief

A concise internal summary of value delivered, adoption signals, stakeholder health, open risks, customer timing, likely objections, and the recommended renewal path.

Value Evidence Summary

The customer-facing narrative that connects the original reason for purchase to First Value, Value Blocks, insights, measurable progress, and the next goal.

Usage & License Review

A practical review of adoption depth, seat utilization, feature usage, underused capacity, workflow consolidation opportunities, and expansion indicators.

Stakeholder & Approval Map

A view of champions, economic buyers, blockers, procurement contacts, legal requirements, executive sponsors, and anyone else who can influence the renewal path.

Expansion Signal Review

A structured assessment of where growth may be warranted based on customer goals, usage patterns, new stakeholders, adjacent teams, and tier or package fit.

Next-Year Value Plan

The forward-looking plan that translates the renewal into the next set of goals, Value Blocks, Alignment Meetings, insight opportunities, and growth milestones.

Keep these assets light enough to use. A readiness brief that takes a full day to build will not scale, and a value summary that reads like a marketing deck will not persuade. Each asset should help answer a few questions: Why did the customer buy? What has changed because of the relationship? Who cares about the outcome? What could block renewal? What growth is justified? What happens next? As the operating system matures, the assets should assemble themselves from the evidence the earlier plays captured, instead of being rebuilt by hand at the end of every year.

Create the AI Layer

Renewal requires synthesis across more signals than any single dashboard shows: usage, stakeholder coverage, goal progress, open risks, commercial history, support themes, procurement timing, and the customer’s own language about value. AI can pull those together faster than a person can, but the purpose is better judgment, not automation for its own sake.

The most useful starting point is the renewal readiness brief. Run a lighter version before the Mid-Year Reframe to see what looks healthy, unclear, or at risk, and the full version as the renewal window approaches.

AI Prompt · Renewal Readiness Brief

Using the customer’s kickoff notes, First Value record, Value Blocks, Alignment Meeting summaries, product usage, support history, stakeholder activity, and commercial details, create a renewal readiness brief. Separate known facts from assumptions. Include the original reason for purchase, progress against goals, adoption patterns, the stakeholder map, unresolved risks, budget or procurement considerations, expansion signals, likely objections, and the recommended renewal or growth path.

AI can also draft the customer-facing value story. This is where judgment matters most. The output should sound like the customer’s progress, not a machine-generated business review, and the CSM should edit heavily.

AI Prompt · Value Evidence Narrative

Draft a customer-facing value evidence narrative for the Renewal & Growth conversation. Use the customer’s own language where available. Connect the original goals to progress made, describe the most important wins, acknowledge any unresolved gaps, explain what has changed in the customer’s business, and recommend the next-year value plan. Keep the tone consultative and avoid marketing language. Use only the evidence provided. Do not invent metrics or results, and flag anywhere the evidence is missing.

Two more uses are worth naming. When AI has access to usage and license data, it can help judge whether expansion is justified: flagging accounts approaching limits or showing interest beyond their package, and just as importantly, accounts that should stabilize or right-size first. It can also help the team prepare for likely objections from finance, procurement, the sponsor, and users, grounded in the account’s actual evidence.

The power of the AI layer is not that it closes the renewal. It helps the team see the renewal earlier, prepare more completely, and connect the commercial ask to the customer’s real experience.

Measure the Renew & Grow Play

The Renew & Grow Play is too important to manage by instinct. The team should know whether renewal expectations were set early, whether the Mid-Year Reframe happened, whether the evidence layer is complete, whether the right stakeholders are engaged, whether risk was identified before the formal window, and whether the next-year value plan existed before the contract closed.

Renewal rate and net revenue retention matter, but they arrive too late to coach the behavior that produced them. Leading indicators tell you whether the team is managing the system or reacting to the result. Did the customer reach First Value? Complete meaningful Value Blocks? Attend Alignment Meetings? Was an executive sponsor engaged? Was the renewal path known before the last ninety days?

Over time, compare accounts that went through the full motion with accounts that did not. Accounts with clear goals, visible evidence, engaged stakeholders, and early planning should renew more predictably and expand more responsibly. The others may still renew, but they will lean on heroics, discounting, or late escalation, which is exactly what the operating system is designed to reduce.

The Three Outcomes

Every Renew & Grow motion ends in one of three outcomes. The customer renews, expands, or leaves. The operating system should be ready for all three.

When the customer renews, the lifecycle resets. Translate the renewal into a next-year value plan, confirm the next goal, and schedule the next Alignment Meeting. A renewal without a reset leaves the customer committed again without knowing what the next chapter is for.

When the customer expands, do not confuse commercial success with customer readiness. Expansion needs a plan for adoption, ownership, and value realization, carried through the next set of plays. The right expansion strengthens the relationship. The wrong one increases the contract before it increases disappointment.

When the customer leaves, the play still matters. Sometimes the product is no longer a fit, the customer changed, the value was not achieved, or the provider made mistakes. Either way, the last interaction often defines how the customer remembers the entire relationship, and that memory shapes referrals, reputation, and the chance they come back. Chapter 17’s supporting plays, Key Contact Change and Offboarding, exist to protect those moments.

When Renew & Grow is weak, the symptoms are familiar: procurement requirements surface late, the champion cannot explain the value internally, usage concerns appear for the first time during negotiation, and Sales enters without context. When it is strong, the customer has been part of the renewal path from early on, value has been visible all year, and procurement timing is known. They are not being asked to believe a story they have never heard. They are being asked to continue a story they helped create.

Designed Outcome

The Renew & Grow Play turns the accumulated evidence of value into commitment, continuation, and the next stage of growth.

The renewal is not the finish line. It is the proof point. When it works, the relationship does not end with a signature. It resets with a stronger foundation and a clearer next goal. Renew & Grow does not simply protect revenue. It turns progress into the next investment.

How the Pipeline Moves

The Renew & Grow play operates at the intersection of all three layers. The plays serve the customer by making value visible and the next goal clear. The renewal has been in the pipeline since the first day of the relationship, advanced stage by stage by every play that came before this one. Here it moves through Intent to Net Revenue Close when the customer confirms a buying path with committed terms, and the expansion opportunities identified along the way move with it. The pipeline tracks both. The renewal is the floor. The expansion opportunities are the growth.
